Research: AI for Energy, Smart Meter Data Analytics and Smart Grids

My research aligns data and artificial intelligence to make electricity systems more efficient, flexible and trustworthy. I work with smart meter and grid time-series data to understand how consumers use electricity, to detect anomalies such as electricity theft and unreported rooftop solar PV or EV chargers, and to turn these insights into demand-side management, demand response and energy conservation strategies.

1. Aligning data and intelligence for smart grid applications

A modern smart grid connects supply (solar, wind, hydrogen, nuclear, hydro), the grid (transmission lines and substations), storage (batteries and electric vehicles) and demand from the residential, commercial and industrial sectors. Power flows between these blocks, but so does data: smart meters, sensors and controllers generate a continuous stream of measurements. Sitting at the centre of this architecture is a data and AI layer that turns those measurements into decisions.

My work follows the pipeline shown at the bottom of the figure: data collection from homes, renewables and the network; monitoring and control; data analytics to find patterns and anomalies; AI-driven decision making; and finally explainable AI (xAI), so that utilities and consumers can understand and trust why a decision was made. This view framed my PhD thesis, Smart Meter Data Analytics: Aligning Data and Intelligence for Sustainable Energy Management (IIT Kharagpur, 2023). The thesis can be requested from the IIT Kharagpur repository; the document is sent to your email by an authorised person from IIT Kharagpur.

2. Is smart meter data analytics useful for energy conservation? A meta-analysis

Utilities around the world have run feedback programmes that use smart meter data to help households save energy, with very mixed results. To find out what actually works, we carried out a meta-analytic review: we defined the review question, designed a search strategy, collected studies from scientific databases, screened them against inclusion criteria, extracted the reported savings and pooled them statistically.

The answer is yes, smart meter data analytics is useful, and the analysis identifies the factors linked with higher savings: energy reports that include a comparison with neighbours, feedback combined with a goal, the programme's sample size and data availability, daily feedback frequency, appliance-specific analysis, and delivery through a web portal or mobile app. These findings give utilities evidence-based guidance for designing energy conservation programmes.

Paper: A meta-analytic approach for determining the success factors for energy conservation, Energy (2021) DOI: 10.1016/j.energy.2021.120821

3. Cluster-specific analysis for demand-side management and net metering

Households do not all use electricity the same way, so one-size-fits-all tariffs and programmes miss a lot of potential. In this framework, thousands of daily smart meter time series are grouped with hierarchical clustering (Ward linkage) into representative load profiles, each compared on weekdays and weekends. Some clusters are almost flat, others have a strong midday peak, and others an evening peak.

Each representative profile is then broken into interpretable components: base load, discretionary load, and morning, daytime, pre-peak, peak and post-peak usage. From these we derive practical indicators such as the number of peaks per day, a suitable rooftop solar size, and the share of consumption during solar generation hours. The result is profile-specific demand-side management (DSM) and net metering (NM) strategies that support informed decisions by both customers and utilities. Related work extends this to the variability of electricity demand for designing demand response and energy efficiency programmes.

Paper: A novel cluster-specific analysis framework for demand-side management and net metering using smart meter data, Sustainable Energy, Grids and Networks (2022) DOI: 10.1016/j.segan.2022.100771
Related: A data-driven analytic approach for investigation of electricity demand variability for energy conservation programs, Energy (2023) DOI: 10.1016/j.energy.2023.128939; and Understanding the electricity demand variability for designing demand response and energy efficiency strategies using smart metering data, 14th International Conference on Sustainable Energy & Environmental Protection "SEEP2022" (2022).

4. Electricity theft and unauthorised solar PV detection from smart meter data

Electricity theft detection (top). Real theft cases are rare, so we start from normal consumption samples and synthesise realistic malicious patterns to build a balanced dataset. For each target day d, the method builds a context-aware profile from the preceding days and compares it with its neighbours using dynamic time warping (DTW), which handles small shifts in timing better than Euclidean distance. A k-nearest-neighbour (kNN) search over the previous 90 days (k = 9, i.e. 10%) gives a distance that is converted to an anomaly score with a Gaussian error function; days above a threshold are flagged as anomalous, the rest as normal.

Solar prosumer and EV detection (bottom). When a household installs rooftop solar PV without informing the utility, the meter only sees net consumption, so the generation is hidden (censored). Using the same context-aware DTW-kNN idea as a change-point detector, each day receives a change-point score; a sharp, sustained jump marks the date the PV system was installed. The approach is statistically verified and also reveals other behavioural changes, such as the introduction of an electric vehicle (EV-4W).

We later extended this into a two-phase approach that first classifies a household as normal, solar PV, EV or PV + EV, and then dates the change. You can try it in the interactive solar PV & EV detector demo.
